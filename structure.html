Product Structure · Port Terminal MVP

How the app is built around a terminal's signals

One coordination core, fed by six kinds of input and read through four screens. Every input, a fault code, an invoice, a voicemail, a photo, becomes the same kind of record.

Plain English A busy port spits out information in a dozen messy forms: sensor alarms, paper sign-off sheets, texts, photos on a worker's phone. This app turns all of it into one standard "something needs attention" note, decides what matters, gets the repair approved, and only marks it done once there's proof the fix actually happened.

Overview

The app is one coordination core fed by six input layers and read through four surfaces. Every input becomes the same signal record, so the core can triage it, gate approval, and verify the fix the same way no matter where it came from.

Watching screens and camera views is just one generic input layer, not the centerpiece. Seed data from the facility's prior maintenance plan, its older maintenance tracker, and its vendor list, plus a cost baseline from the historical expense workbooks, gives version 1 something real to run on from day one.

The one rule

Every layer lands in signal intake first. Only the coordination core creates, approves, and closes work.

Operations context is the underrated layer. A prior facility maintenance plan required the yard cleared before vessel operations and certain water lines drained before a freeze. Weather and the vessel schedule turn those notes into automatic triggers and "don't dispatch now" windows.

The observation harness is one generic pattern: point it at a screen region or a camera view, define what normal looks like, and report when the state changes. A dark light fixture, a red lamp on an equipment panel, and a card reader dropping offline in the access-control system are the same adapter with a different configuration.

Field reports: the creative channels

These channels need no integration contracts, and they hold most of a mid-market terminal's real maintenance data today.

  • Phone photos. Timestamp and location place each photo in a yard zone. On-device text recognition reads painted equipment numbers, container numbers, hour meters, and dashboard fault codes. A before-and-after pair becomes verification evidence. A shared maintenance album files the photos people already take.
  • Document scanner for paper that stays. End-of-shift sign-off sheets, loading sheets from the guard shack, and the work-request form get snapped and turned into structured records, without a fight with crews over paper.
  • Maintenance phone line. Voicemail is transcribed into a draft work request; caller ID identifies the reporter. This covers vendors and operators who don't have the app.
  • Text message. "Unit 153 leaking coolant" plus a photo, texted to one number.
  • Email inbox. Forwarded quotes and invoices are parsed and tagged to an asset automatically, which clears the uncategorized expense rows in the baseline.
  • Tap tags on each machine. An NFC or QR tap identifies the asset and fills the equipment-tag field from the old work-request form. It also proves the pre-shift check happened at the machine.
  • Voice shortcut. Hands-free voice report with location, for people wearing gloves.
  • Phone depth-scanning. Measures pothole depth under container stacks that prior notes flagged to monitor; repeat measurements give a trend line.
  • In-app equipment feedback form. Operator requests feed the requirement library.
  • Later, carefully: maintenance-radio transcription. Needs union and site-security agreement before anything is recorded.

Field reports are the noisiest input. Intake treats them as low-confidence until matched to an asset, and merges duplicates when three people photograph the same leak.

The core and its surfaces

Every input becomes one signal record: source and layer, asset match and match confidence, timestamp and location, attached evidence. The core turns signals into work orders behind a port-authority-style approval gate, then closes them only on proof: an after photo, a device back online, a clean reading.

Painkiller baseline: what v1 must beat

The historical expense workbooks show a reactive-heavy operation that ran over budget, with a meaningful share of spend untraceable to any asset. Version 1 reproduces every one of these findings automatically.

Approval latency and time-to-repair aren't in the historical workbooks. They are the numbers that prove the coordination is worth paying for, so we capture both from the first day.

Phasing by integration cost

Layers are sequenced cheapest-to-connect first, so version 1 ships with zero vendor integrations.
