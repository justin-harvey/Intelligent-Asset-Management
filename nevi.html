The Funded Driver

The NEVI uptime mandate, in detail

Our whole demand thesis rests on one federal rule. Here is exactly what it requires, how it is measured, and why it converts "maintenance" into a funded compliance obligation.

The rule in one line

Each federally funded charging port must maintain an average annual uptime above 97%, calculated monthly on a trailing-12-month basis, under 23 CFR 680.116(b) (final rule, Federal Register, 28 Feb 2023). The obligation runs for 5 years of operation.

What counts as "up"

A port is "up" only when its hardware and software are online and available for use (or in use) AND it actually dispenses electricity at the required minimum power level. A charger that powers on but won't deliver a charge is counted as down. That definition is the point: it makes successful restoration, not "we reset it," the thing that matters, which is exactly what our verify step confirms.

How uptime is calculated

μ = ( ( 525,600 − ( Toutage − Texcluded ) ) / 525,600 ) × 100
  • 525,600 = minutes in a year (the denominator).
  • Toutage = total minutes the port was down.
  • Texcluded = minutes excused under the rule (outages outside the operator's control, per the defined exclusions).

To allow verification, FHWA requires reporting both underlying fields, Toutage and Texcluded, in addition to the uptime metric itself. In other words, you must show your work.

The reporting burden (which is itself a product surface)

Chargers and networks must securely measure, communicate, store, and report:

  • Energy and power dispensed
  • Real-time charging-port status
  • Real-time price to the customer
  • Historical charging-port uptime (with Toutage / Texcluded)

Assembling defensible, audit-ready uptime evidence across mixed hardware is a chore operators do today by hand. Our evidence ledger produces it as a byproduct of coordinating the repair.

Enforcement and the 5-year obligation

NEVI requires operation and maintenance to standard for 5 years. Enforcement flows through the state subrecipient agreements, not a flat federal fine: states can require remediation, withhold or claw back funds, and condition future awards on compliance.

Honest note on "penalties"

The federal rule sets the requirement; it does not publish a universal dollar penalty. The real exposure is state-by-state, remediation demands, funding clawback, and reputational/award risk, plus the lost revenue of a dead charger. We should quantify the specific state's mechanism with each operator in validation (see M6, Test 4), not assert a single national number.

Why this funds us

The mandate hands us three things at once
  • A funded budget line, uptime is now a compliance obligation with money behind it, not a nice-to-have.
  • A named owner, someone is accountable for the 97% number (our economic buyer).
  • A measurable target and a reporting requirement we can satisfy as a byproduct.

And the gap is wide open: hitting 97% needs an O&M/coordination layer the CPMS doesn't provide, while real-world first-time charging success sits around 71% (roughly one attempt in three fails). The mandate is the demand; the reliability gap is the problem; no incumbent maintenance system exists to close it.
