Is the cost overrun normal?

How a reactive-heavy terminal's maintenance spend compares with published benchmarks, and which numbers survive a buyer's diligence.

Plain English Before we claim a terminal wastes money on repairs, we checked the public research. Short version: running mostly "fix it when it breaks" is normal and below best practice; poor maintenance really does drive big costs at ports; but the flashy "reactive costs 3 to 5 times more" stat is unsourced, so we don't use it.
Bottom line up front

The terminal's mix is typical of a reactive-heavy operation, not an outlier. Its preventive share sits near the industry norm and far below best-practice targets.

The overrun itself has no public benchmark. No credible source reports how often maintenance budgets are exceeded, so a mid-teens-percent overrun stands as our own data point.

The reactive cost premium is real but smaller and less certain than usually claimed. Lead with the port-specific insurer figures and drop the "3 to 5 times" line.

What the reactive premium really is

Published estimates of how much more reactive repairs cost run from 1.4 times to over 10 times, and the most-quoted figure has no traceable source.

ClaimWhere it comes fromStatus
"3 to 5 times" / "3 times"Widely attributed to a US Department of Energy best-practices guideDoes not appear in the guide it cites. Avoid it.
1.4 times to over 10 timesVarious secondary sourcesWide and inconsistent; not defensible as a single number
Up to 6 timesPort-and-terminal insurer claims dataPort-specific and traceable; use this

The Department of Energy figures trace to a single pump-industry source, unchanged across three editions of the guide, and the 3-times comparison is against a full reliability-centered program, not ordinary preventive maintenance.

Port-specific evidence (the strongest source)

A transport-and-logistics insurer's claims data is the strongest port source, because it ties poor maintenance directly to equipment loss.

  • About a quarter of equipment-damage cost at ports and terminals came from routine claims caused by inadequate or incorrect maintenance.
  • Roughly half of quay-crane storm claims, where cranes were blown along their rails, were made worse by poorly maintained gantry motors or brakes.
  • Up to 6 times the cost of planned maintenance for an unexpected failure and repair, in the insurer's own estimate.
  • Around 15% maintenance savings over a decade at one medium-sized terminal on a preventive service contract, per a vendor case study (not an industry average).
  • 15 to 30 minutes of partial terminal shutdown from a single machine breakdown in the yard, in a worst case.

Downtime is getting more expensive

A 2024 industry report found unplanned-downtime costs in heavy industry rose 319% from 2019 to 2023, against 19% US inflation. It covers large manufacturers, not ports, so use it for direction rather than a port dollar figure.

Some port-software vendors quote "$50,000 an hour" for quay-crane downtime. None of the pages checked cite a source, so it is left out here.

How to use this in the pitch

Say

"The terminal's historical numbers are typical of a reactive-heavy operation: preventive work in the low-40s percent against best-practice targets of 80%+, and an insurer's claims data ties about a quarter of port equipment-damage cost to poor maintenance."

Avoid

"Reactive maintenance costs 3 to 5 times more, according to the Department of Energy." A diligent buyer can trace it and find it isn't there.

Whether overruns are common is a question for validation Test 1: ask each of the five operators for their last fiscal year's maintenance budget, actual spend, and preventive share.
